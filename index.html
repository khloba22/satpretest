Chapter 1 • 6 Subtopics (~35% of Exam)

Algebra: Linear Equations, Systems & Inequalities

Subtopic 1.1

Multi-Step Linear Equations & Constants ($kx + c = d$)

Isolating variables with unknown parameters $kx + c = d$ requires structured inverse operations. To solve for $x$ when parameters are present, treat letters like $k$ as constants: subtract $c$ from both sides ($kx = d - c$), then divide by $k$ (assuming $k \neq 0$) to get $x = \frac{d - c}{k}$. On the Digital SAT, these items frequently test your ability to determine for what value of $k$ an equation has unique solutions, no solution, or infinite solutions. Always check for constraints where denominators or coefficients become zero.

Subtopic 1.2

Slope-Intercept ($y=mx+b$) & Standard Form ($Ax+By=C$)

Linear equations appear in two primary formats on the SAT: Slope-Intercept Form ($y = mx + b$), where $m$ is the constant rate of change (slope) and $(0, b)$ is the initial $y$-intercept; and Standard Form ($Ax + By = C$), where the slope is given directly by $m = -\frac{A}{B}$, the $x$-intercept is $\left(\frac{C}{A}, 0\right)$, and the $y$-intercept is $\left(0, \frac{C}{B}\right)$. Converting seamlessly between these two forms enables rapid graphing and parameter identification.

Subtopic 1.3

Parallel ($m_1=m_2$) & Perpendicular ($m_1m_2=-1$) Lines

Geometric relationships between linear graphs depend entirely on their slopes. Parallel lines share identical slopes ($m_1 = m_2$) and never intersect, representing inconsistent systems with zero solutions. Perpendicular lines intersect at a strict $90^\circ$ right angle and possess negative reciprocal slopes ($m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}$ or $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$). To write a perpendicular line equation through a given point $(x_1, y_1)$, determine the negative reciprocal slope and apply point-slope form: $y - y_1 = m_{\text{perp}}(x - x_1)$.

Subtopic 1.4

Linear Modeling: Interpreting Slope & $y$-Intercept in Context

In real-world applications modeled by $y = mx + b$, the $y$-intercept $b$ represents the fixed initial amount when the independent variable $x = 0$ (e.g., startup costs, base membership fee, or initial height). The slope $m$ represents the constant rate of change per unit increase in $x$ (e.g., cost per mile driven, hourly wage, or growth rate per month). Read word problems carefully to ensure your units match the slope denominator and numerator.

Subtopic 1.5

Systems of Linear Equations (0, 1, or $\infty$ Solutions)

A system of two linear equations $a_1x + b_1y = c_1$ and $a_2x + b_2y = c_2$ can yield three distinct outcomes: (1) One unique solution when slopes differ ($\frac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \frac{b_1}{b_2}$); (2) Zero solutions (inconsistent) when slopes are equal but $y$-intercepts differ ($\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \frac{c_1}{c_2}$); and (3) Infinitely many solutions (dependent) when both equations represent the exact same line ($\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}$). Use substitution or elimination when solving numerical systems.

Subtopic 1.6

Linear Inequalities & Shaded Feasible Regions

Linear inequalities describe half-plane regions in the coordinate plane. Use a solid boundary line for inclusive inequalities ($\le$ or $\ge$) and a dashed boundary line for strict inequalities ($<$ or $>$). To determine which side to shade, test an easy coordinate point—such as $(0, 0)$—in the inequality. If the resulting statement is true, shade the region containing $(0, 0)$; if false, shade the opposite side. Systems of inequalities form overlapping polygon "feasible regions" where all constraints hold true.

Chapter 2 • 9 Subtopics (~35% of Exam)

Advanced Math: Quadratics, Polynomials, Radicals & Exponentials

Subtopic 2.1

Exponent & Radical Laws ($x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m}$)

Mastering exponent rules is essential for manipulating algebraic expressions. Key laws include product of powers ($x^a \cdot x^b = x^{a+b}$), quotient of powers ($\frac{x^a}{x^b} = x^{a-b}$), power of a power ($(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$), negative exponents ($x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}$), and fractional exponents bridging radicals ($x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m} = (\sqrt[n]{x})^m$).

Subtopic 2.2

Polynomial Arithmetic & Difference of Squares

Adding, subtracting, and multiplying polynomials requires careful distribution of negative signs and combination of like terms. Memorize high-frequency polynomial expansions: difference of squares ($a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$) and perfect square trinomials ($(a \pm b)^2 = a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2$). Recognizing these patterns allows you to factor expressions instantly without prolonged trial-and-error.

Subtopic 2.3

Quadratic Formula & Discriminant ($\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$)

For any quadratic equation in standard form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, the roots are given by the quadratic formula $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. The expression under the square root, $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ (the discriminant), reveals root nature: when $\Delta > 0$, there are 2 distinct real solutions; when $\Delta = 0$, there is exactly 1 real solution (vertex on the axis); and when $\Delta < 0$, there are 0 real solutions (complex roots).

Subtopic 2.4

Parabola Vertex, Axis of Symmetry & Vieta's Formulas

Quadratic functions appear in standard form ($ax^2 + bx + c$), vertex form ($a(x-h)^2 + k$), and factored form ($a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$). The vertex $(h, k)$ gives the maximum or minimum value. The axis of symmetry is $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$. Vieta's formulas state that for $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, the sum of roots is $r_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a}$ and the product of roots is $r_1 r_2 = \frac{c}{a}$.

Subtopic 2.5

Rational & Radical Equations (Extraneous Roots)

Solving rational equations (fractions with variables in denominators) requires multiplying by the common denominator to clear fractions. Solving radical equations requires isolating the radical and squaring both sides. Both techniques can generate extraneous solutions—algebraic values that solve the manipulated equation but violate domain restrictions or make denominators zero in the original equation. Always substitute answers back to verify!

Subtopic 2.6

Absolute Value Equations ($|ax+b|=c$) & V-Graphs

Absolute value equations $|ax + b| = c$ (where $c \ge 0$) split into two separate linear equations: $ax + b = c$ and $ax + b = -c$. Graphically, $y = a|x - h| + k$ forms a symmetrical V-shape with its vertex at $(h, k)$. If $c < 0$, an absolute value equation has no solution because absolute values can never yield negative distances.

Subtopic 2.7

Nonlinear Systems (Line-Parabola & Circle Intersections)

Nonlinear systems combine linear equations with quadratics, circles, or polynomials. Solve them using substitution: isolate $y$ in the linear equation and substitute it into the nonlinear equation. The resulting single-variable equation can then be factored or checked with the discriminant to determine how many intersection points exist (0, 1, 2, or more).

Subtopic 2.8

Exponential Growth & Decay Models $f(t)=a(1\pm r)^{t/p}$

Exponential models follow $f(t) = a(1 \pm r)^{t/p}$, where $a$ is the initial value at $t = 0$, $r$ is the decimal rate of growth ($+$) or decay ($-$), and $p$ is the length of time per compounding or growth period. Pay close attention to phrasing like "triples every 4 hours" ($b = 3, p = 4$) or "decreases by $15\%$ annually" ($b = 0.85$).

Subtopic 2.9

Function Notation $f(g(x))$ & Graph Translations

Function notation requires evaluating inside-out: for $f(g(3))$, compute $g(3)$ first, then plug that output into $f(x)$. Graph transformations modify parent functions $f(x)$: adding $c$ outside ($f(x) + c$) shifts the graph vertically upward; replacing $x$ with $(x - c)$ shifts the graph horizontally to the right by $c$ units.

Chapter 3 • 8 Subtopics (~15% of Exam)

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis: Statistics, Probability & Scatterplots

Subtopic 3.1

Ratios, Rates & Proportions

Ratios compare quantities with the same units, while rates compare quantities with different units (e.g., miles per hour). Direct proportions state that $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$, solved via cross-multiplication ($ad = bc$). Inverse proportions state that $x \cdot y = k$, meaning as one variable increases, the other decreases proportionally.

Subtopic 3.2

Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis converts units by stringing together fractional conversion factors. Set up equations so intermediate units cancel diagonally (numerator to denominator). For example, converting miles per hour to feet per second requires multiplying by $\frac{5280\text{ ft}}{1\text{ mile}}$ and $\frac{1\text{ hr}}{3600\text{ sec}}$.

Subtopic 3.3

Percentages & Sequential Changes

Percentage problems involve percent increase ($1 + r$) and percent decrease ($1 - r$). When calculating reverse percentages (e.g., finding original price after a $20\%$ discount resulted in $\$64$), divide by the multiplier ($P \cdot 0.80 = 64 \implies P = 80$). Sequential percent changes multiply compounding multipliers rather than adding percentages.

Subtopic 3.4

Mean, Median & Standard Deviation

The mean (average) is sensitive to extreme outliers, whereas the median (middle value) is resistant. Standard deviation measures typical data spread around the mean: tightly clustered data yields a small standard deviation, while widely dispersed data yields a large one. Adding a constant to every data point shifts the mean but leaves the standard deviation unchanged.

Subtopic 3.5

Box-and-Whisker Plots & IQR

Box plots display the five-number summary: Minimum, Lower Quartile ($Q_1$, 25th percentile), Median ($Q_2$, 50th percentile), Upper Quartile ($Q_3$, 75th percentile), and Maximum. The Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$) measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data and is unaffected by extreme outliers.

Subtopic 3.6

Scatterplots, Best Fit & Residuals

Scatterplots display paired numerical data. The line or curve of best fit generates predicted values ($\hat{y}$). A residual represents the vertical distance between an actual data point and the model prediction: $e = y_{\text{actual}} - \hat{y}$. Positive residuals mean the model underestimated the actual value; negative residuals mean the model overestimated.

Subtopic 3.7

Probability & Contingency Tables

Two-way contingency tables organize categorical data into rows and columns. Conditional probability restricts the sample space to a specific sub-category: $P(A \mid B) = \frac{\text{Number in both } A \text{ and } B}{\text{Total number in } B}$. Always check whether the denominator is the grand total or a specific row/column total.

Subtopic 3.8

Random Sampling & Generalizability

Study conclusions depend heavily on design. Random sampling allows results to be generalized to the broader population from which the sample was drawn. Random assignment to treatment and control groups allows researchers to establish cause-and-effect (causal) relationships. Without random assignment, only correlation can be claimed.

Chapter 4 • 8 Subtopics (~15% of Exam)

Geometry & Trigonometry: Right Triangles, SOH-CAH-TOA & Circles

Subtopic 4.1

2D Area, Perimeter & $k^2$ Scaling

Standard area formulas include triangles ($A = \frac{1}{2}bh$), rectangles ($A = \ell w$), circles ($A = \pi r^2$), and trapezoids ($A = \frac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)h$). When two 2D geometric figures are similar with a linear scale factor $k$, their perimeters scale by $k$, but their areas scale by $k^2$.

Subtopic 4.2

3D Volume, Surface Area & $k^3$ Scaling

Volume formulas include rectangular prisms ($\ell wh$), cylinders ($\pi r^2 h$), cones ($\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$), and spheres ($\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$). For similar 3D solids with linear scale factor $k$, surface areas scale by $k^2$, while volumes scale cubically by $k^3$. Density is computed as $\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$.

Subtopic 4.3

Parallel Lines & Transversals

When a transversal intersects two parallel lines, 8 angles are formed that are either equal (acute angles equal acute angles, obtuse equal obtuse) or supplementary (sum to $180^\circ$). Key angle pairs include corresponding angles, alternate interior angles, and consecutive interior angles.

Subtopic 4.4

Congruent & Similar Triangles

Triangles are similar if their corresponding angles are equal, yielding proportional side lengths ($\frac{a}{d} = \frac{b}{e} = \frac{c}{f}$). Similarity criteria include Angle-Angle (AA), Side-Angle-Side (SAS), and Side-Side-Side (SSS). Congruent triangles share identical side lengths and angle measures.

Subtopic 4.5

Pythagorean Theorem & Special Triangles

In any right triangle, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. Memorize common Pythagorean triples ($3\text{-}4\text{-}5$, $5\text{-}12\text{-}13$, $8\text{-}15\text{-}17$) for instant calculation. Also memorize special right triangle ratios: $30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangles have side lengths $x, x\sqrt{3}, 2x$, and $45^\circ\text{-}45^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangles have leg ratios $x, x, x\sqrt{2}$.

Subtopic 4.6

SOH-CAH-TOA & Complementary Angles

Trigonometric ratios are $\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$, $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$, and $\tan(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}$. A high-yield SAT identity states that if two acute angles $A$ and $B$ are complementary ($A + B = 90^\circ$ or $\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians), then $\sin(A) = \cos(B)$ and $\cos(A) = \sin(B)$.

Subtopic 4.7

Circle Equation & Completing the Square

The standard equation of a circle is $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$, where $(h, k)$ is the center and $r$ is the radius. When given in general form ($x^2 + Ax + y^2 + By + C = 0$), use completing the square on both $x$ and $y$ terms to transform the equation into standard form and extract the center and radius.

Subtopic 4.8

Arc Length, Sectors & Inscribed Angles

Arc length $s$ and sector area $A$ are proportional fractions of the total circumference and circle area: $\frac{s}{2\pi r} = \frac{\theta^\circ}{360^\circ}$ (or $s = r\theta$ with $\theta$ in radians). The Inscribed Angle Theorem states that an inscribed angle is always exactly half the measure of the central angle that subtends the same arc.
